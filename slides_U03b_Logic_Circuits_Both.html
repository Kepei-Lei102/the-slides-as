Watch one gate become three

① NOT — tie the inputs together
A Ā
NAND(A, A) = NOT A — because A·A is just A, then the bubble flips it.
A=0→1 · A=1→0 ✓
1 NAND = 1 NOT. (Turing Complete's opening level — some of you have done this with your own hands.)
② AND — flip the flip
A B A·B
A NAND is an AND that lies. So make it lie twice: NAND, then the NOT trick on its output.
2 NANDs = 1 AND.
③ OR — De Morgan's re-costume
A B A+B
Flip both inputs, then NAND them: "NOT(Ā·B̄)" — and De Morgan says that IS "A OR B". You shaded this law in maths class.
3 NANDs = 1 OR.
The clincher: every circuit you will ever draw is made of AND, OR, NOT — and each of those is just NANDs. So the factory prints ONE shape, millions of times. The die photo finally makes sense. And Turing Complete hands you one gate at the start because one gate is enough — the game's opening is this theorem in costume.

🕳️ beyond the exam

The other universal gate — and the Moon

🌗 NOR is universal too.
Run the same three constructions with NORs instead of NANDs — they all work (the mirror of the proof you just saw). And that's not hypothetical: the Apollo Guidance Computer — the machine that landed people on the Moon — was built from one chip type: a dual 3-input NOR gate, thousands of identical chips, wired into everything. One shape. It flew.
⚡ So why does modern silicon pick NAND?
Recall the cellar from last week: in CMOS, the gate's series stack sits on the fast transistors in a NAND — and electrons outrun holes roughly 2–3× — so NAND is the smaller, quicker worker. One repeatable pattern = cheaper factories, fewer defects, higher yield. NOR got the Moon; NAND got everything after.
Universality is the theorem. Which universal gate wins is economics.

English → expression

"The alarm sounds if the window sensor and the motion sensor both fire, or if the panic button is pressed."

"both fire" → W AND M
 "or if panic" → (…) OR P
 A = (W AND M) OR P
Why the brackets are not optional: W AND M OR P could mean W AND (M OR P) — a different alarm (one that needs the window every time). Precedence: NOT first, then AND, then OR — and brackets make the order explicit. On the exam, when in doubt: bracket.

Write it down before you look — the answer is on the next slide.

Your turn — the answer

"The pump X runs if the tank is not full (F) and the switch S is on."

X = (NOT F) AND S

The brackets are not decoration. NOT F AND S is ambiguous to a reader and wrong to a machine that applies NOT last — it would test "not (F and S)", which is a different pump. Negate the one thing you meant to negate, and bracket it.

For the winners — and for the machines

🏆 The prize
Crack the 3-house version and you get the original: 5 houses, 5 nationalities, 5 drinks, 5 hobbies, 5 pets — 15 interlocking clues. The question is still the fish. It is printed, it is yours, and it is not going on this screen.

This is a real job. Chip designers ask "does this circuit ever give the wrong answer?" — that's the same puzzle with billions of houses. The tools that answer it are called SAT solvers, and they run the modern world's hardware checks.

Expression → circuit

A = (W AND M) OR P — now build it:

The method — start from the middle of the sentence:
① Find the operation outside all brackets — it sits in the middle of the line. That is the final gate, drawn right at the output.
② Each side of that operator is a smaller expression — same rule again, working leftward.
③ When you hit plain letters, those are the input wires. It always works.
Drawing conventions (marks live here): inputs enter on the left · output leaves on the right · a junction dot wherever one wire feeds two gates · and on 0478: every gate max two inputs — three things ANDed = two AND gates chained.
P OR A W M AND

the OR is outside all brackets — so it went down first, at the output; the bracket became the AND that feeds it

Circuit → expression (reading in reverse)

A B C X XOR NOR P

two gates, one intermediate wire

The last gate is the middle of the sentence.
① The gate touching X is a NOR — so the answer already has its shape: X = ( … ) NOR ( … ), NOR in the middle.
② Fill the blanks from the wires that feed it — name them: P = A XOR B; the other is just C.
③ Substitute: X = (A XOR B) NOR C — works on any circuit, however deep.
The bubble trap: NOT (A AND B) and (NOT A) AND B are different circuits. Where the bubble sits decides what gets flipped — the whole gate's output, or one input. Brackets are how you say which.

Working backward: table → circuit

Someone hands you only a fingerprint and demands the circuit:

ABF
001
010
100
111
The recipe:
① Find every row where the output is 1.
② Describe each such row with ANDs — put a NOT on every input showing 0.
③ OR the row-descriptions together. Done.
Row 0 0 → 1:  (NOT A) AND (NOT B)
Row 1 1 → 1:  A AND B

F = ((NOT A) AND (NOT B)) OR (A AND B)
— "both off, or both on": you just built an equality detector, XOR's twin.
The theorem you just used: any truth table you can dream up — any behaviour at all — has a circuit. A circuit always exists. This is why the four languages are really one language.
But count the 1s first — the small-table shortcut: two 1s → XOR · one 1 → AND (1 sits at row 1 1) or NOR (at 0 0) · three 1s → OR or NAND (find the lone 0). This table has two 1s — but in XOR's wrong seats (0 0 and 1 1). None of the six fit. That's how you know it's recipe time.

The pro's version

The recipe is honest but bloated.
Read the burglar alarm backward off its table and you describe all five firing rows: five 3-input ANDs, a 5-input OR, plus NOTs — a dozen-plus gates for the alarm we built this hour with two.
There is an algebra that dismantles the bloat.
One glimpse: (W·M·P) OR (W·M·NOT P) — two terms differing in exactly one letter — merge into W·M, and the letter vanishes. Rule by rule, twelve gates collapse back to two. There's even a visual tool that does it by eye.
That algebra — and the tool — is A2 material (9618 §15.2). For now, know this: the recipe guarantees a circuit exists; the algebra makes it cheap. The door closes here. 🚪

The plant pays. Last week, a hole ended with: "1⊕1 = 0… which is what you write for 1+1. Hold that thought." You held it for a week. XOR writes the sum. AND raises the carry. Binary addition — the thing from U2 — is these two gates.
And it scales. Give it one upgrade (a carry-in, so columns can chain) and copies of this circuit add numbers of any width. Your calculator, your phone, the machine showing this slide — adding by pure logic, billions of times a second.
The week's arc closes: physics forced two symbols (U3a) → two symbols made gates → gates make arithmetic. A computer doesn't do maths despite being made of logic. Arithmetic IS logic.

The five classic traps: ① brackets dropped — precedence decides a different circuit · ② bubble scope: NOT(A AND B) ≠ (NOT A) AND B · ③ a branching wire with no junction dot (or an input drawn twice) · ④ a 3-input gate drawn where the paper says max two · ⑤ English "or" read as XOR
Through the marker's eyes: a hand-drawn gate is identified by its tail — the back edge, where the inputs enter. Straight tail → AND family; curved tail → OR family (XOR wears a second arc). The nose proves little — draw the tail like you mean it. And wires only connect where you plant a junction dot; a crossing without a dot is two strangers passing.

Everything you built today… forgets

Every circuit from this lesson is a reflex: change the inputs, the output follows instantly — and the past is gone. Nothing in it can hold anything.

But your machine remembers. Your save file. Your high score. This very slide, sitting in RAM.

The cellar: a loop with a mind of its own

ONE inverter in a loop — "my output is my own opposite." No stable answer exists, so it never settles. Watch:
0

flip. flip. flip. forever. The liar's paradox in silicon — and engineers sell it as a clock.

TWO inverters in a loop — "the opposite of my opposite." Stable, twice over — it holds whatever you left in it:
1 0

calm. That is a bit, remembering — your CPU's fastest memory is exactly this.

Stop there. How you write into the loop — and the trap when two writes collide — is a lesson of its own, and it's a good one. 🚪
