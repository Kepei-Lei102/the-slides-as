9618 §2.1 — the characteristics of a LAN and a WAN

LAN and WAN — the sentences, not the acronym

THE CLASSROOM'S LAN — N21/11 · J23/12 · a recent paper (the scheme's words)
  1. Covers a small geographical area — one room, one building, one site [1]
  2. The infrastructure is privately owned — the school's own cables, switches, access points; nothing leased, and the internet is not needed to send within the building [1]
  3. A high data transfer rate — say rate, not "faster" [1]
  4. Wired or wireless — "all LANs are wired" is a recent paper's misconception

Benefits are a different question (J23/12): sharing files and data · sharing hardware and software · central management (backup, security). The report: benefits confused with characteristics, and "gives each computer internet access" — nothing in the question said internet.

THE SCHOOL'S TWO SITES — A WAN (J23/13 · J25/12 · N24/13)
  1. Covers a large geographical area — two sites, a city, the world [1]
  2. Uses external / public / leased infrastructure — non-dedicated hardware, a telecoms company's lines [1]
  3. Connections are often virtual rather than physical; a lower transfer rate; harder to secure [1]
  4. A WAN is not "wireless" — the J25 misconception

A difference names both sides. "A WAN covers a large area and a LAN a small one" scores; "a LAN is faster" does not — "the rate of transfer of data in a LAN is greater" does (J23 report).

Reality note — is the boundary blurring? In practice, yes. A VPN makes a teacher's laptop at home behave as if it were on the school LAN, over the public internet; cloud services put the "server" a continent away while the app feels local; a campus Wi-Fi mesh stretches one LAN across a square kilometre. What still separates them — and what the scheme pays for — is who owns the infrastructure (yours, or leased from a telecoms company) and how far it physically reaches. A VPN is a WAN wearing a LAN's addresses, and the exam still calls it a WAN.
9618 §2.1 — bus, star, mesh, hybrid; how packets travel in each; justify a topology ▶ live

Four shapes, one packet — the posting board and the postman

9618 J24/11 Q8(a) — the tick table · N23/11 Q2(d) — star over bus · J25/12 Q6(d) — draw it

The four, on the card — and what the scheme pays for each

Compare shapes; label the drawing

bus — the posting board
all devices to one central cable · every transmission is broadcast, only the addressed device keeps it · one at a time, so collisions · the cable fails, the network fails · cheap, hard to scale · ✗ privacy: everyone hears
star — the postman
all devices to one central device (a switch) · the sender sends to the switch, the switch checks the destination address and sends only to the intended receiver · ✓ no shared-medium collisions on switched full-duplex links; easy to add a node, one cable fault isolates one device · ✗ the switch is the single point of failure
mesh — many roads
A full mesh links every pair; a partial mesh uses selected links. Alternative paths can survive a failed link. Full mesh: n(n−1)/2 links. Duplex and protocol determine collisions; mesh alone does not.
hybrid — the school
any combination — stars joined by a backbone is nearly every real LAN · in the reviewed archive, no standalone hybrid question was found; practise it when a scenario joins different network shapes
Star drawing check: include every requested device and label; connect each directly to the switch. Add no unrequested intermediary.

9618 §2.1 — the client-server and peer-to-peer models; roles; justify for a situation

Who asks, who answers — a server at the front, or twenty equals

CLIENT-SERVER — J24/11 Q5(a), the bank's app; J25/13 Q3(c), the browser game
  1. Name the server in the scenario: the bank's server · the game's web server [1]
  2. Its role: stores all the customer / game data; receives the requests, processes them, sends the response [1]
  3. Name the client in the scenario: the customer's smartphone running the app · the player's web browser — a device or a program, never the person [1]
  4. Its role: sends requests, waits, displays the response [1]

The J24 report: "'the server is the bank server and the client is the customer' is insufficient"; "many responses described the actions of the smartphone user." The J25 report: the browser is the client, "sometimes incorrectly identified as the server".

PEER-TO-PEER — J21/11 Q4, Melinda and her friends
  1. All computers are of equal status — no central server [1]
  2. Each computer provides access to its own resources and data — the data is distributed [1]
  3. Each is responsible for its own security [1]

Drawbacks need a reason (two marks each): reduced security — because there is no central management, and it is only as secure as the weakest computer · no central backup — if one computer's data is not backed up it is lost to all · files are only available while the computer that holds them is switched on.

The J21 report: "just 'weak security' is not enough"; and "considerable confusion between a peer-to-peer network and a mesh topology" — a model is who serves whom; a topology is how the cables run.

The classroom decides. The twenty computers and the server at the front are client-server: the server holds the files and the accounts, the twenty ask. Two friends sharing a folder over the Wi-Fi are peers. The same twenty computers, wired as a star, could run either model — the shape and the model are two different questions, and the exam asks them separately.
9618 §2.1 — thin-client and thick-client and the differences between them

Thin or thick — where the data lives, where the work is done

THIN CLIENT — J24/12 Q3(b) (the exam markers) · J23/13 Q2(c) · a recent paper
  1. Data is not stored on the client — the papers stay on the server
  2. The client is reliant on access to the server / the connection — the marker cannot mark when the server is down
  3. It needs few local resources — a low-specification laptop will do
  4. It performs minimal processing — sends requests, displays results; the server does the work

The table's second column is the mark: "data is not stored on the client" scores nothing until it says the exam papers are stored on the server, not on the examiner's computer. And the converse-pair rule: "the server does most of the processing" then "the client does very little" is one characteristic, scored once.

THICK CLIENT — J23/12 Q1(e)
  1. The client does most of its own processing, independently
  2. Its resources are installed locally — the software, the storage
  3. The server performs minimal / some processing for it
  4. It works when the network does not

The J23 report: a thick-client answer needs "at least one statement about the role of the client and another about the role of the server"; "many responses were too vague and could apply to any client-server model."

The classroom: twenty thin clients are cheap, centrally managed, and dead when the server is; twenty thick clients cost more, need updating one by one, and keep working when the server goes down.

Reality note — are thin and thick blurring? Yes. A browser app that caches and works offline, a phone that runs its own AI models but keeps the data in the cloud, a console game whose world lives on a server: most modern software sits between the two ends. The exam's pairs are the two ends of a spectrum, and the marks are for saying which end a given system leans to and why — where is the data, where is the processing, what dies without the connection.

🕳 Three drawings, 1964 — the shape that survives

Paul Baran, RAND Corporation, On Distributed Communications, 1964 — the figure every network textbook still redraws.

A star, a hybrid of stars, a mesh. Baran's question was not a classroom's: which shape of communications network survives an attack on its centre? The centralised one dies with its centre; the decentralised one loses a region; the distributed one — every node joined to three or four neighbours, messages cut into blocks and relayed by whichever road is still standing — keeps working with half its nodes gone. The ARPANET's first four nodes (1969) were wired as his mesh, and the internet still is: a mesh of meshes.
And the classroom chose the drawing he rejected. A star: one switch, twenty cables, a single point of failure — chosen because nobody is bombing the classroom, a switch costs fifty pounds, and 210 cables would not. That is the tick table's last two rows read as history: robust if any line fails is the mesh, and it is why the internet has no centre to strike; one central device is the star, and it is why your room has one.
The message cut into blocks is the other half of Baran's paper — the packet, and the switching of packets, which the A-level teaches in A12. Tonight: the shape.
