Two ladders, and they drift apart

Engineer's (×1024)Shopkeeper's (×1000)gap
KiB = 1 024 BkB = 1 000 B2.4 %
MiB = 1 048 576 BMB = 10⁶ B4.9 %
GiB = 2³⁰ BGB = 10⁹ B7.4 %
TiB = 2⁴⁰ BTB = 10¹² B10.0 %
PiB = 2⁵⁰ BPB = 10¹⁵ B12.6 %
EiB = 2⁶⁰ BEB = 10¹⁸ B15.3 %
The gap is not fixed. It compounds. Every rung multiplies the disagreement again, because you are comparing 1024ⁿ with 1000ⁿ.
Which is why nobody complained in 1970. At kilobyte scale the two ladders are 2.4 % apart — that is 24 bytes, and 24 bytes was not worth an argument. Drives grew. The argument arrived on schedule.
Exam rule, in the syllabus's own words: "Calculations must use the measurement of 1024 and not 1000." In this room, and in that hall, the engineer's ladder is the only one you compute with.
⬇ but why 1024? 🕳️
🕳️ beyond the exam

Why 1024?

Memory is not a shelf you can build to any length. To reach a cell you send it an address — in binary, down a fixed number of wires. n wires can name exactly 2ⁿ cells. There is no chip in the world with 1000 addressable cells, because you cannot get 1000 out of n wires.
10 wires → 2¹⁰ → 1024 cells. Which is almost a thousand. So the engineers reached for the word they already had, and called it a kilobyte. They were off by 24 bytes and nobody in 1965 cared.
And the near-miss is a fact about logarithms. log₁₀2 = 0.30103 — so ten doublings land on 103.0103, a hair over three decades. That 0.0103 is the whole ambiguity. A rounding error in a logarithm became an industry's argument.
You will meet those address wires again for real, when we open the processor and count them.
⬇ then why do drives use 1000? 🕳️
🕳️ beyond the exam

Why your RAM is binary but your disk is not

🧠 RAM has no choice.
Address wires, 2ⁿ cells. A stick sold as 8 GB genuinely holds 8 GiB — 8 589 934 592 bytes. Nobody is rounding; the hardware could not have come out any other number.
💽 A hard disk has a choice.
A platter is addressed by sector number, and a sector count can be anything at all. Nothing forces a power of two — so the number on the box became a marketing decision, and marketing picked the ladder with the bigger number.
⚡ An SSD is the interesting one.
Flash chips are built on the binary ladder. A drive sold as 256 GB physically contains 256 GiB of silicon — 274.9 decimal GB. It sells you 256 × 10⁹ and keeps the rest.
The controller keeps those ≈ 18.9 GB deliberately. They are its over-provisioning: a private reserve of spare blocks, used to keep the drive fast and to retire cells as they wear out. You never see them and you cannot have them. On a hard disk the gap is bookkeeping; on an SSD it is a workshop.
And look at the ratio one more time. 274.9 ÷ 256 = 1.0737 — the same 2³⁰ ÷ 10⁹ that made Windows say 238, turning up again at the other end of the same drive. Wherever the two ladders touch, that number is waiting.
⬇ so did anyone actually sue? 🕳️
🕳️ beyond the exam

Yes. In court.

⚖️ Western Digital, settled 2006
A class action on behalf of roughly a million buyers. The complaint's example: a drive sold as 80 GB that Windows reported as 74.5 GB. WD settled by giving out free backup software valued at $30 a copy. Seagate faced the same claim — that its drives were overstated by 7 %.
📐 Look at that 7 % again.
It is not a number a lawyer chose. It is the giga row of the table two slides back: 2³⁰ ÷ 10⁹ = 1.0737. The lawsuit was, in the end, an argument about which of two ladders the word "giga" was standing on.
And this is where your syllabus's strange vocabulary comes from. In 1998 the IEC published a fix: new prefixes for the binary ladder — kibi, mebi, gibi — where the "bi" says binary, so that "kilo" could go back to meaning 1000 and nobody would ever have to argue again.
Almost nobody uses them. Twenty-eight years later, the industry still says "GB" for both. Cambridge, to its credit, insists on the honest spelling — which is why your exam is one of the few places on earth you will read the word mebibyte.

Settle it 🐍

The box sold me 256 000 000 000 bytes. That is what "256 GB" means on the shopkeeper's ladder, and the drive delivered every one of them.

Windows then counted those same bytes on the engineer's ladder — divide by 1024 three times to reach gibibytes — and printed the answer with the label "GB".

238 GB and 256 GB are the same drive. Nothing is missing. One number is bytes ÷ 10⁹; the other is bytes ÷ 2³⁰. The bytes never moved.

Two ladders, one drive, one line of arithmetic each. Try a 1 TB drive: 1000000000000.

🧾 Outside the exam hall

Every so often I will stop and tell you what a piece of this syllabus is actually worth once the exam is over. Here is today's ledger.

WHAT TRANSFERS
The ×1024 / ×1000 split — permanently. The moment a number looks about 7 % off, "which kind of giga?" should be your first thought, and it will be right more often than any other guess. It is not only disks: network speeds are decimal, and they are in bits. A "100 Mbps" line carries 100 000 000 bits per second, which is 100 000 000 ÷ 8 ÷ 1024 ÷ 1024 ≈ 11.9 MiB/s — and that is why a download you were promised at "100 megabits" arrives at "12 megabytes" and nobody has cheated you.
WHAT DOESN'T
Saying "mebibyte" out loud. Almost nobody does. In twenty-eight years the words have not caught on outside standards documents, exam papers and a corner of the Linux world — engineers write GB, mean either one, and work out which from context. One place does spell it your way: the KDE desktop writes KiB / MiB / GiB in its file manager, while GNOME writes kB / MB / GB. Two Linux desktops, same files, different words — which tells you the argument is still live.
So: learn the words for the exam, and keep the arithmetic for life. That is not a complaint about the syllabus — the syllabus is teaching the honest version. It is a warning that if you say "gibibyte" at your first job, somebody will look at you strangely, and you will still be the one who is right.

How big is an image?

It is counting, not physics. An image is a grid of pixels. Every pixel stores the same number of bits. So:
width × height × colour depth and colour depth is in bits, so that total is in bits
Then convert, one rung at a time. ÷8 to reach bytes. ÷1024 to reach KiB. ÷1024 again to reach MiB. Never divide by 8000, and never by 1000.
9618 says estimate; 0478 says calculate. That is not carelessness. A real image file also carries a file header — width, height, colour depth, which format it is — before the first pixel. It is small, and no exam expects you to size it, but it is why the honest word is "estimate".

A 16-bit colour image, 512 pixels wide by 512 high. Give the file size in KiB.

pixels512 × 512 = 262 144 bits262 144 × 16 = 4 194 304 bytes4 194 304 ÷ 8 = 524 288 KiB524 288 ÷ 1024 = 512 KiB
Every one of those lines is a mark. The real mark scheme gives up to two marks for the stages of working and one for the final answer — so a correct number with no working is one mark out of three.

The size machine 🐍

Same four lines you just did by hand. Run it — then change one number and run it again.

① Double the width to 1024. What happens to the size? Predict before you press run.
② Drop the depth from 16 to 8. Prediction first, again.
③ Now a real one: a 4000 × 3000 photo at depth 24. Answer in MiB — you will need one more / 1024.
Your syllabus states this as a fact to memorise: "the file size increases as the resolution and colour depth increase." You have just made it happen twice. It is not a fact about images; it is what multiplication does.

How big is a sound?

Same idea: count the numbers. A recording measures the sound thousands of times a second, and stores each measurement in a fixed number of bits.
sample rate × sample resolution × length samples per second × bits per sample × seconds → bits
Two things the exam formula quietly leaves out. Real audio is usually stereo — two channels, so twice the samples, so twice the size. And like an image, a sound file carries a header. Neither board will ask you for them; both are why your answer is a floor, not a ceiling.
📐 WHERE WE LEAVE THE SYLLABUS'S ORDER
Your syllabus prints storage units, file-size calculation and compression together in one section, and we are doing only the first two today. Compression comes later, with images and sound properly — because today's lesson is arithmetic in the right units, and compression is a different idea that deserves its own hour rather than a footnote after it.

A 10 second sound effect, sample rate 22 016 Hz, sample resolution 8 bits. Give the file size in KiB.

samples22 016 × 10 = 220 160 bits220 160 × 8 = 1 761 280 bytes1 761 280 ÷ 8 = 220 160 KiB220 160 ÷ 1024 = 215 KiB
Watch the middle two lines. ×8 then ÷8 — because the resolution was 8 bits. The numbers 220 160 appear twice and mean different things: once samples, once bytes. Label your lines and this can never bite you.
⬇ so why is my music file so small? 🕳️
🕳️ beyond the exam

Then why is my photo only 3 MB?

Your phone shoots 12 megapixels at 24-bit colour. Run our formula: 12 000 000 × 24 = 288 000 000 bits ÷ 8 ÷ 1024 ÷ 1024 = 34.3 MiB.
Go and look at the actual file. It is about 3 MB. Nine tenths of it is gone — and the picture still looks like the picture.
Everything you calculated today is therefore an upper bound: how big the file would be if nobody had been clever. Somebody was extremely clever, roughly a thousand times, and the results are called JPEG, PNG, MP3, and about forty others.
How do you throw away 90 % of a photograph and still have a photograph? That is a whole unit of its own, and it is one of the genuinely beautiful things in this subject. Not today.

Drill — exam wording, exam speed

1. State how many bytes there are in a kibibyte (KiB). [1]
1024
2. Give the name of the data storage measurement that is equal to 1024 gibibytes (GiB). [1]
tebibyte (TiB)
3. An image has a resolution of 1000 × 1000 and a colour depth of 2 bytes. Calculate the file size in bytes. [2]
1000 × 1000 = 1 000 000
1 000 000 × 2 = 2 000 000 bytes
4. A bitmap image is 2048 pixels wide by 1024 high, with a bit depth of 10 bits per pixel. Estimate the file size in mebibytes. [2]
2048 × 1024 × 10 = 20 971 520 bits
÷ 8 = 2 621 440 bytes
÷ 1024 ÷ 1024 = 2.5 MiB
Question 3 is the trap of the topic. The depth was given in bytes, not bits — so there is no ÷8. Anyone who divided by 8 out of habit has just handed back seven eighths of the file. Read the unit on every number before you touch it.
